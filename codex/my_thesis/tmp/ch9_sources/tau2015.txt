   Reconstruction, Energy Calibration, and Identification of
Hadronically Decaying Tau Leptons in the ATLAS Experiment for
                       Run-2 of the LHC

                                          The ATLAS Collaboration




                                                     Abstract
       The reconstruction algorithm, energy calibration, and identification methods for hadronically
       decaying tau leptons in ATLAS used at the start of Run-2 of the Large Hadron Collider are
       described in this note. All algorithms have been optimised for Run-2 conditions. The en-
       ergy calibration relies on Monte Carlo samples with hadronic tau lepton decays, and applies
       multiplicative factors based on the pT of the reconstructed tau lepton to the energy meas-
       urements in the calorimeters. The identification employs boosted decision trees. Systematic
       uncertainties on the energy scale, reconstruction efficiency and identification efficiency of
       hadronically decaying tau leptons are determined using Monte Carlo samples that simulate
       varying conditions.

1 Introduction

This note describes the updates in the reconstruction, identification and energy calibration of hadronically
                                                                            √
decaying tau leptons in ATLAS [1], introduced for the beginning of the s = 13 TeV run of the Large
Hadron Collider (LHC) [2]. It summarizes the changes with respect to the methods used during the
√
  s = 8 TeV run [3] and provides the expected performance based on simulation. Systematic uncertainties
on the energy scale as well as on the reconstruction and identification efficiency are evaluated based on
simulation studies and on the experience from previous data-taking periods.
Performance changes are expected mainly from the addition of a new layer of the pixel detector, the IBL [4],
which improves the impact parameter and tau decay vertex resolution, as well as from the calorimeter
readout configuration, updated for operation with 25 ns bunch spacing. Discrimination against electrons
has been harmonized with the electron identification algorithm, instead of implementing a dedicated veto.
In addition there were many updates of a technical nature, due to the developments of the ATLAS software
framework.
After a description of the ATLAS detector in section 2, the simulated samples used for the optimization,
determination of performance, and evaluation of the systematic uncertainty are described in section 3.
Reconstruction of the hadronic component of tau decays (in the following indicated as τhad−vis ) is outlined
in section 4. The discrimination of hadronic tau decays from the main backgrounds, hadronic jets and
electrons, is discussed in section 5, while section 6 presents the energy calibration and resolution.


2 ATLAS detector

The ATLAS detector [1] consists of an inner tracking system surrounded by a superconducting solenoid,
electromagnetic (EM) and hadronic (HAD) calorimeters, and a muon spectrometer (MS).
The inner detector (ID) is immersed in a 2 T axial magnetic field, and consists of pixel and silicon
microstrip (SCT) detectors inside a transition radiation tracker (TRT), providing charged-particle tracking
                                   √
in the region |η| < 2.51. For the s = 13 TeV run, a fourth layer of the pixel detector, the IBL [4], has
been inserted at an average radius of 33.2 mm, providing an additional position measurement with 8 µm
resolution in the (x, y) plane and 40 µm along z.
The EM calorimeter (ECAL) uses lead as the absorber and liquid argon (LAr) as the active material. In
the central rapidity region, the EM calorimeter is divided into three layers, one of them segmented in
highly granular η strips for optimal γ/π 0 separation, and completed by a presampler layer for |η| < 1.8.
Hadron calorimetry is based on different detector technologies, with scintillator tiles (|η| < 1.7) or LAr
(1.5 < |η| < 4.9) as the active medium, and with steel, copper, or tungsten as the absorber material. The
calorimeters provide coverage within |η| < 4.9.
The MS consists of superconducting air-core toroids, a system of trigger chambers covering the range
|η| < 2.4, and high-precision tracking chambers allowing muon momentum measurements for |η| < 2.7.

Physics objects are identified using their specific detector signatures; electrons are reconstructed by
matching a track from the ID to an energy deposit in the calorimeters [5, 6], while muons are reconstructed
using tracks from the MS and ID [7]. Jets are reconstructed using the anti-k t algorithm [8] with a distance

using a local hadronic calibration [9], serve as inputs to the jet algorithm.


3 Event samples and sources of systematic uncertainties

The τhad−vis reconstruction, identification and calibration are tuned using Z/γ ∗ → ττ events simulated
using Powheg-Box v2 [10] interfaced to the Pythia 8.186 [11] parton shower model. The CT10 PDF
set [12] is used in the matrix element. The AZNLO [13] tune is used, with the PDF set CTEQ6L1 [14], for
the modeling of non-perturbative effects. The EvtGen v1.2.0 program [15] is used for the modelling of the
bottom and charm hadron decays. Photos++ version 3.52 [16] is used for QED emissions from electroweak
vertices and charged leptons. Generated events are processed through the ATLAS detector simulation [17]
based on GEANT4 [18]. The Monte Carlo (MC) simulation samples consist of an inclusive Z/γ ∗ → ττ
sample, while high-mass Drell-Yan τ + τ − samples cover the high-pT part of the spectrum.
The identification algorithms are optimized using background samples of di-jet events, simulated using
Pythia 8.175 [11] with AU2 tune [19] and CT10 PDF [12], and of Z/γ ∗ → ee events with the same
generator settings as for the inclusive Z/γ ∗ → ττ sample.
The nominal samples are produced with the expected ATLAS configuration and conditions for the 13 TeV
operation with 25 ns bunch spacing. The pileup due to multiple pp interactions per bunch crossing
is simulated by adding event-by-event a number of minimum bias interactions extracted from a Poisson
distribution with average number of interaction per bunch crossing µ. The value of µ follows a distribution
covering the range of values expected during the 2015 data-taking, with a mean value about 25. The
interactions of particles with the detector are simulated by GEANT4 using the FTFP_BERT hadronic
shower model.
The following systematic uncertainties on the performance have been evaluated using dedicated Z/γ ∗ →
ττ samples generated with different configurations:
    • Uncertainty on the inner detector material, which affects track reconstruction efficiency, photon
      conversion rate and the amount of energy loss in front of the calorimeter, has been studied by
      increasing the passive material in the inner detector by 5%. A more precise modeling of the
                                             √
      detector material, using early data at s = 13 TeV, has been used in recent publications [20, 21],
      but it has been checked it doesn’t have a sizeable effect on the uncertainty quoted on this systematic.
    • Uncertainty in the calorimeter performance and calibration have been estimated by varying the
      noise thresholds in the reconstruction of TopoClusters. Both upwards and downwards variations
      have been considered and the absolute value of the maximum deviation is quoted as the systematic
      uncertainty.
    • Uncertainty due to underlying event tunes are derived by comparing the nominal sample with a
      sample simulated using the A14 tune [22].
    • Events generated with the QGSP_BIC GEANT4 hadronic shower model are used to evaluate
      uncertainties on the modeling of interaction with the detector.

In addition, uncertainties due to the pileup mismodeling have been evaluated by dividing the nominal
sample into four µ bins and taking half of the difference between the uppermost and lowermost bin.
Systematic uncertainties are evaluated in different |η| regions as a function of the τhad−vis candidate pT ,
after the calibration described in section 6.


4 Reconstruction

The τhad−vis reconstruction algorithm used in the 2012 data-taking has been deployed with minor modific-
ation for the 13 TeV data-taking. It is described in Ref. [3] and summarized in the following. Jets formed
using the anti-k t algorithm with a distance parameter R = 0.4 and clusters of calorimeter cells, calibrated
using a local hadronic calibration (LC) as inputs, are used as seeds of the τhad−vis reconstruction algorithm
if they satisfy pT > 10 GeV and |η| < 2.5.
The τhad−vis pT at the LC scale is set to the total energy of TopoClusters within ∆R < 0.2. This is the
energy scale used in the identification variables of section 5.1. This is also the base value for the final
energy calibration discussed in section 6.1.
In τhad−vis reconstruction, the hadronic component (HAD) of the TopoClusters consists of the cells in the
last layer of the electromagnetic calorimeter and of the hadronic calorimeter, while the electromagnetic
(EM) component is referred to the energy deposited in the other layers of the ECAL.
The performance of the efficiencies and calibration in this note are evaluated for τhad−vis candidates with
|η| < 2.5, outside the transition region 1.37 < |η| < 1.52 between the barrel and forward calorimeters,
and with a reconstructed pT > 20 GeV, after the calibration described in section 6.


4.1 Vertex association

In events with multiple simultaneous interactions, the chosen primary vertex does not always correspond to
the vertex at which the tau lepton is produced. To reduce the effects of pile-up and increase reconstruction
efficiency, the tau lepton production vertex is identified, among the previously reconstructed primary
vertex candidates in the event.
The tau vertex (TV) association algorithm uses as input all tau candidate tracks in the region ∆R < 0.2
around the jet seed direction. The pT of these tracks is summed and the primary vertex candidate to which
the largest fraction of the pT sum is matched is chosen as the TV [23].
New impact parameters can then be recalculated with respect to the TV and τhad−vis tracks are selected
using the criteria described in Section 4.2. This vertex is used to determine the τhad−vis direction, to
associate tracks, and to build the coordinate system in which identification variables presented in section 5
are calculated.
In Fig. 1, the TV performance is compared to the general purpose algorithm used in ATLAS, which selects
the primary vertex that maximize the sum of pT2 of associated tracks. The TV provides a better efficiency
for the reconstruction of low-pT τhad−vis candidates, especially in a high pile-up environment.

4.2 Track selection

Tracks are associated to the τhad−vis candidate following the same criteria used in Run-1. They are
required to be in the core region ∆R < 0.2 around the τhad−vis direction and to satisfy the following
criteria: pT > 1 GeV, at least two associated hits in the pixel detector (including the IBL), and at
least seven hits in total in the pixel and the SCT detectors. Furthermore, requirements are imposed on
the distance of closest approach of the track to the TV in the transverse plane, |d 0 | < 1.0 mm, and
longitudinally, |∆z0 sin θ| < 1.5 mm. This selection is optimized to maximize the fraction of 1-prong and
3-prong tau decays reconstructed with the correct charged particle multiplicity.
The distribution of the number of tracks associated to τhad−vis candidates, separately for 1-prong and
3-prong decays, is displayed in Fig. 2. The leading cause of underestimation of the number of prongs
is tracking inefficiency due to hadronic interactions in the inner detector, while the leading cause for
overestimation of the number of prongs comes from photon conversion tracks that pass the track selection
criteria.

4.3 Reconstruction efficiency and systematic uncertainties

The reconstruction efficiency is defined as the fraction of 1-prong (3-prong) hadronic tau decays which
are reconstructed as 1-track (3-track) τhad−vis candidates.
The detector is almost fully efficient for finding a jet-seed for a τhad−vis candidate with pT > 20 GeV within
the pseudorapidity acceptance. Track and vertex selections, described in sections 4.1 and 4.2 therefore
play the dominant role for the reconstruction efficiency shown in Fig. 3. The reconstruction efficiency
is relatively constant for 1-prong decays, with a slow drop at high momentum due to two effects. Very
high-pT tau leptons may decay after the first pixel detector and fail the requirement on the number of
hits. In addition, the probability to wrongly classify an electron from photon conversions as a charged
hadron from tau decays also increases with pT and results in an incorrect assignment of the number of
charged particles in the tau decay. For 3-prong decays there is a reduction in the low-pT bins, due to the
minimum transverse momentum requirement on the charged decay products, and at high-pT due to the
increased collimation of the decay products that results in an increased probability to miss a track because
of overlapping trajectories.

The relative systematic uncertainties on the reconstruction efficiency are summarized in Fig. 4. The
effect of the systematics variations is found to be uniform along η and the sum of all contributions is in
the range between 2% and 4.5%. The main sources of systematic uncertainties are from pileup and the
inner detector material. The former affects the efficiency mainly through the TV, where the probability
to incorrectly identify the τhad−vis candidate production vertex depends on the amount of interactions
reconstructed in the event, as shown in Fig. 1(b), and is especially relevant for low-pT 1-prong decays.
The latter impacts directly the track counting, as the number of reconstructed conversion tracks and the
efficiency to reconstruct pion tracks can both be affected by the amount of detector material.

The identification of τhad−vis candidates to discriminate tau lepton decays from hadronic jets follows the
same general approach used in the 2012 data-taking and is described in Ref. [3]. Separate Boosted
Decision Trees (BDT) algorithms are trained for 1-track and 3-track τhad-vis decays using the samples
listed in section 3: Z/γ ∗ → ττ for signal and di-jet events for background.
One notable difference compared to the 2012 data-taking is that the identification variables and pile up
correction procedure are now uniform between offline reconstruction and trigger. Part of this harmoniza-
tion was the replacement of the π 0 reconstruction algorithm described in section 3.1 of Ref. [3] with some
of its input variables and up to two EM TopoClusters for the π 0 component. This variable choice provides
an equivalent level of rejection compared to the original algorithm.

5.3 Discrimination against electrons

While the BDT classifier discussed in section 5.2 was trained in order to distinguish hadronic tau decays
from hadronic jets, it provides insufficient discriminating power between electrons and 1-prong hadronic
tau decays. As charged pions provide one of the major backgrounds to the reconstruction of electrons, a
powerful Likelihood discriminator between the two has been built in the context of the ATLAS electron
reconstruction and identification effort [25]. This discriminator is based on both shower shape information
from the calorimeter measurements as well as on reconstructed hits in the tracking detector, including
information from transition radiation in the TRT.
In order to exploit this Likelihood discriminator for the discrimination between hadronic tau decays and
electrons, a geometrical matching within a cone of ∆R < 0.4 is performed between reconstructed tau



                                                                                                  14
candidates and electrons with pT > 5 GeV. Such a matching is necessary because there is no one-to-one
correspondence between tau candidates and electron candidates. Reconstructed 1-prong tau candidates
which are matched to an electron candidate of a large Likelihood score value are rejected. The cut values
on the electron Likelihood score are parametrised as a function of η trk and pT to offer a constant tau
efficiency of 95% with respect to a sample of tau candidates passing the Loose identification working
point.
During electron reconstruction, loose requirements are applied on basic shower shape observables such
as the hadronic leakage of energy deposits in the electromagnetic into the hadronic calorimeter. Due to
these requirements, not all reconstructed tau candidates can be matched to electron candidates and are
assigned an artificial Likelihood value of -4, corresponding to a very tau-like classification.
Figure 13(a) shows the distribution of the Likelihood score for a sample of true 1-prong hadronic tau
decays and a sample of true electrons from Z → ττ and Z → ee decays, respectively. Figure 13(b) shows
the discriminating power of this classification algorithm in terms of the inverse background efficiency as
a function of signal efficiency in different bins of η trk .
As the electron Likelihood discriminator was optimised to provide stable performance in differing pileup
scenarios, this feature is directly inherited to the electron rejection algorithm in the context of hadronic tau
identification. Figures 14(a) and 14(b) show the signal efficiency as a function of pT and µ, respectively:
both are within 1% of the working point in high-statistics bins.
In order to estimate the systematic uncertainty on the signal efficiency of this procedure, the efficiency
is compared between the different, systematically varied simulated event samples of section 3 and the
nominal simulation. The effect of the individual variation and the total uncertainty obtained from their
sum in quadrature are presented in Fig. 15. For pT < 100 GeV, uncertainties are below 2%, but increase
at higher pT reaching a value of 2.5 − 5% depending on the pseudorapidity.

6 Energy calibration

As mentioned in section 4, the tau energy scale calibration developed for the start of Run-2 uses calorimeter
information only. After the reconstruction step, the energy of tau candidates is calibrated at the LC scale,
which mostly corrects for the calorimeter non-compensation and for the energy deposited in dead material
or outside TopoClusters.
The tau energy scale consists of two additional corrections which calibrate the tau energy back to the
true visible energy. First, the energy contribution originating from pileup interactions is subtracted. A
response correction is then applied to account for the following possible effects for the τhad−vis particles:
decay products not reaching the calorimeter, not depositing enough energy to create TopoClusters, or not
detected within ∆R = 0.2 of the reconstructed τhad−vis candidate. The τhad−vis momentum is computed
from the energy assuming zero mass.

closure of the calibration in the nominal sample, which corresponds to the deviation from unity of that
same Gaussian mean, is also taken into account. The pileup uncertainty, which accounts for the remaining
dependence of the calibrated energy on the average number of interactions per bunch crossing µ, is found
to be less than 1%. The residual dependence with respect to the number of primary vertices NPV is of
the same order. The underlying event tune has a negligible impact on the calibration. The uncertainty
on the response is evaluated using the alternative Geant4 hadronic shower model, the alternative material
modelling and the variation of the calorimeter noise thresholds. The alternative shower model can affect
the clustering of energetic cells into TopoClusters, as well as the classification of TopoClusters into
electromagnetic/hadronic categories (which affects the LC-calibrated energy). The amount of material in
front of the calorimeter affects both the dead-material correction weights (part of the LC calibration) and
the tau response R. The noise threshold variations estimate the sensitivity of the response to the modelling
of the calorimeter calibration in the simulation.
A more refined and exhaustive treatment of systematic uncertainties on the response was performed in
Run-1 [3]. However, the results of the particle deconvolution method [26] (which takes advantage of
single-pion response measurements in low-µ data and test beam data) are not yet available for Run-2.
While the Run-2 MC-based response uncertainty partly covers the components entering the single-particle
response uncertainty, it does not account for the uncertainty on the EM-scale calibration (π 0 → γγ). As
the detector has not dramatically changed between Run-1 and Run-2, the Run-1 single-particle response
uncertainty has been included as an additional systematic uncertainty on the Run-2 tau energy scale,
inflated by a safety factor of 2. For 1-prong tau decays with |η| < 0.8, the single-particle response
uncertainty is valid up to pT ≃ 500 GeV. Beyond this, test beam data can no longer be used to constrain
the charged-pion response.

7 Conclusion

The reconstruction, identification and energy calibration of hadronically decaying tau leptons has been
                                                                                √
updated for the LHC Run-2 data taking conditions with centre-of-mass energy s = 13 TeV and 25 ns
bunch spacing.
The algorithms have been tuned on simulated samples and provide three nominal identification operation
points with increasing background rejection. The combined reconstruction and identification efficiencies
for these operating points are 0.6 (0.5), 0.55 (0.4) and 0.45 (0.3) for 1-prong (3-prong) tau decays.
Rejection against electrons is achieved using a special working point of the offline electron identification
tool, providing 95% efficiency and an inverse background efficiency between 20 and 200, depending on
the pseudorapidity of the τhad−vis candidate.
Systematic uncertainties have been evaluated before the start of data-taking using simulated samples
corresponding to changes to the underlying event tune, the simulation of the hadronic shower in the
calorimeter, a varied model of the ATLAS geometry and material distribution, and a variation in the
noise thresholds of the calorimeter clustering procedure. They depend on the |η|, pT and charged particle
multiplicity of the τhad−vis candidates. They are in the range 2−4.5% for reconstruction efficiency, 5−18%
for the identification efficiency, 1 − 5% for electron rejection and 3 − 6% for the energy scale.
