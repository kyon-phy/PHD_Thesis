The ATLAS Collaboration
A search for leptoquark signals is performed in final states with a hadronically decaying
𝜏-lepton and missing transverse momentum, using data from proton–proton collisions at a
centre-of-mass energy of√𝑠=13 TeV recorded by the ATLAS detector at the Large Hadron
Collider during Run 2 (2015–18), corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 140 fb−1. The
analysis is designed to probe both resonant production and non-resonant𝑡-channel exchange
of the leptoquark, covering a wide range of coupling scenarios. No excess above the Standard
Model background prediction is observed. Limits are set on the couplings in the benchmark
𝑈1 vector-leptoquark model at 95% confidence level for masses between 1.5 TeV and 3.0 TeV.

1 Introduction
Semileptonic decays of𝐵 mesons provide one of the most sensitive probes of lepton flavour universality
(LFU) in the Standard Model (SM) of particle physics, as well as possible contributions from physics
‘beyond the SM’ (BSM). In particular, a combination of the BaBar [1, 2], Belle [3–6], Belle-II [7, 8] and
LHCb[9–12]resultsshowadeviationofabout 3.8𝜎 fromtheSMexpectationusingtheHFLAVCKM2025

ℓ=𝑒, 𝜇 (ℓ=𝜇 for LHCb) [13], motivating scenarios with new interactions in the𝑏→𝑐𝜏𝜈 transition.
SuchextensionsoftheSMcouldbedescribedbyaleptoquark(LQ)[14–17],ahypotheticalscalarorvector
boson that couples directly to a lepton and a quark, thereby mediating interactions between the lepton and
quark sectors. The𝑅(𝐷 (∗) ) anomaly can be accommodated by sizeable contributions from both the𝑏𝜏
and𝑐𝜈couplings of a LQ [18–20].
A comprehensive programme to search for LQ signals with𝜏-leptons in the final state [21–26] was
developed by the ATLAS [27] and CMS [28] experiments at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC). In LQ
pair production, the cross-section depends only on the strong coupling constant at leading order, making
the searches largely insensitive to the specific choice of the LQ coupling. Signals with LQ masses below
approximately 1.6TeVwere excluded by such searches [21, 23]. Searches for singly produced LQs that
decay into𝑏𝜏 were performed in the𝑏𝜏𝜏 final state, assuming they were produced in association with a
𝜏-lepton. Ifthelepton–quarkcouplingsarelarge, 𝑡-channelexchangeoftheLQcanleadtomoreeventswith

searches for LQ signals in the𝜏𝜏 final state. For the𝑈1 LQ model, the region with𝜆𝑏𝜏 /𝑚U1 ≳1TeV −1

The previous ATLAS and CMS searches in𝜏𝜏(+𝑏) final states mentioned above assumed that the signal
rate depends only on the𝑏𝜏 coupling, because the𝑐𝜈 coupling contributes only through subleading effects.

2 ATLAS detector
The ATLAS detector [27, 30] is a multipurpose apparatus with nearly4𝜋 coverage in solid angle.1 It
consists of an inner tracking detector (ID) inside a2T superconducting solenoid, electromagnetic and
hadronic calorimeters, and a muon spectrometer. The inner tracking detector covers the pseudorapidity
range |𝜂|<2.5 . It consists of silicon pixel, silicon microstrip, and transition radiation tracking detectors.
Lead/liquid-argon (LAr) sampling calorimeters provide electromagnetic (EM) energy measurements with
high granularity within the region|𝜂|<3.2 . A steel/scintillator-tile hadronic calorimeter covers the central
pseudorapidity range (|𝜂|<1.7 ). The endcap and forward regions are instrumented with LAr calorimeters
for EM and hadronic energy measurements up to|𝜂|=4.9 . The muon spectrometer (MS) surrounds the
calorimeters and is based on three large superconducting air-core toroidal magnets with eight coils each.
The field integral of the toroids ranges between2.0 and 6.0Tm across most of the detector. The MS
includes a system of precision tracking chambers up to|𝜂|=2.7 and fast detectors for triggering up to
|𝜂|=2.4 . A two-level trigger system is used to select events [31]. The first-level trigger is implemented in
hardware and uses a subset of the detector information to accept events at a rate close to100kHz. This
is followed by a software-based trigger that reduces the accepted rate of complete events to1.25kHz on
average in Run 2, depending on the data-taking conditions. A software suite [32] is used in data simulation,
in the reconstruction and analysis of real and simulated data, in detector operations, and in the trigger and
data acquisition systems of the experiment.

6 Event selection
Four signal regions (SRs) are defined in order to maximise the sensitivity of the analysis: two for
non-resonant LQ production and two for resonant LQ production, illustrated by the Feynman diagrams
shown in the left and right panels of Figure 1, respectively. Each region is further subdivided according to
the presence or absence of a𝑏-jet, as summarised in Table 2. During the optimisation of the SRs, using
simulated events, the selection thresholds were chosen by maximising the expected exclusion sensitivity,
while requiring the MC statistical uncertainty in the expected background to remain below 30%. The jet
multiplicity requirements suppress top-quark background, while the remaining kinematic requirements
target the resonant or non-resonant signal topology. Events in the SRs are required to pass the𝐸miss
T trigger.
Exactly one𝜏had with 𝑝T >200GeV is required. Events with additional light leptons (electrons or muons)
are vetoed.
Resonanceandnon-resonanceSRsareprimarilydistinguishedbythejet 𝑝T. TheresonanceSRs, SR0b-Res
and SR1b-Res, with no or one𝑏-jet in each event, are optimised for signals with lower LQ masses
(∼1.5 TeV). Events must satisfy𝐸miss
T >200GeV and 𝑚T >200GeV , where𝑚T is the transverse mass
of the𝜏had and 𝐸miss
T . Up to four jets are allowed per event. InSR0b-Res, the number of𝑏-jets must be
zero, and the leading jet’s𝑝T must exceed 250GeV. The invariant mass of the leading jet and the𝜏had,
denoted by𝑚 𝑗 𝜏had, is used as a proxy for the leptoquark mass and is required to be greater than 800GeV.
In SR1b-Res, exactly one𝑏-jet is required, and its𝑝T must exceed 250GeV. In addition, the invariant
mass of the𝑏-jet and the𝜏had, denoted by𝑚𝑏𝜏had, is required to be greater than 800 GeV.
The non-resonance SRs,SR0b-NonResand SR1b-NonRes, target the high-𝑚T regime with either no or
one 𝑏-jet, and hence𝑚T >600GeV and 𝐸miss
T >400GeV are required. The azimuthal separation between
the 𝜏had and 𝐸miss

background, at most two jets are allowed per event. InSR0b-NonRes, the leading jet’s𝑝T is required to
satisfy50< 𝑝 T <250GeV, while forSR1b-NonRes, the𝑏-jet’s𝑝T is used instead.

8 Systematic uncertainties
Experimental and modelling systematic uncertainties are included as normalisation and shape variations in
the fit and are smaller than the statistical uncertainty from the limited SR event yields.
The uncertainty in the combined 2015–2018 integrated luminosity is 0.83% [78], obtained using the
LUCID-2 detector [79] for the primary luminosity measurements, complemented by measurements using
the inner detector and calorimeters.
The 𝜏-lepton energy scale and its uncertainties are measured in situ using𝑍→𝜏𝜏 events, combined with
test beam and MC simulation comparison in the high-𝑝T region [68, 80, 81]. The correction applied
to the simulated𝜏 energy scale is typically below 1% of the reconstructed𝜏had energy. The associated
uncertainty in the absolute𝜏 energy scale is smaller than 1.5%. The efficiencies of𝜏-lepton reconstruction
and identification in the simulation samples are corrected to agree with data, depending on𝑝T, using𝑡¯𝑡
events. The central value of the correction factor is close to unity in all𝑝T ranges, with uncertainties of
5%–8% (6%–11%) for 1-prong (3-prong)𝜏had at the ‘Tight’ identification working point. The uncertainties
in the electron and muon reconstruction and identification efficiencies and their energy and momentum
scales and resolutions are considered, and they are significantly smaller than those affecting𝜏-leptons.
Thejetenergyscaleandresolutionand 𝐸miss
T soft-termuncertaintiesareestimatedusinginsitumethods[76,
82]. The efficiency of𝑏-jet identification in the simulation is corrected to match that measured in𝑡¯𝑡 data
events, and uncertainties in the correction factor are estimated [83–85]. For signal samples using AtlFast3
detector simulation, an additional uncertainty taking into account how it differs from the fullGeant4
simulation is considered, and its impact on the signal yield is around 1%.
Uncertainties in the theoretical modelling of simulated events are also considered. For simulated𝑊+jets
events, variations of the renormalisation and factorisation scales, by factors of 2 and1/2, and the PDFs
are considered [86]. Additionally, approximate NLO electroweak corrections are applied to the nominal
𝑊+jets samples, and the corresponding effect is taken as an additional systematic uncertainty. Since the
normalisationof 𝑊→𝜏𝜈 eventsisestimatedinthe CRWregionswitha 𝑏-jetvetoandappliedtoSRswith
a 𝑏-jet, a 30% uncertainty is assigned in those regions to the ratio of𝑊-boson production in association
with heavy-flavour hadrons (𝑊+HF) to that with light-flavour hadrons (𝑊+LF), according to previous
ATLAS studies [87–90]. The𝑊+HF modelling uncertainty is decorrelated between the resonant and
non-resonant regions.
For simulated top-quark events, variations of the ME calculation, renormalisation and factorisation scales,
and PDFs are considered, similarly to the𝑊+jets samples, along with variations related to initial- and
final-state radiation and theℎdamp parameter [91]. The difference in the top-quark𝑝T distribution’s shape
between the nominal NLO prediction and the NNLO prediction [92] is also considered as an additional
uncertainty. Uncertainties in the interference between𝑡¯𝑡 and single-𝑡 events are estimated by comparing
simulations using the diagram removal and diagram subtraction schemes [93].
The background containing a jet misidentified as a𝜏had is a minor contribution in the SRs and is mainly
due to𝑍→𝜈𝜈 production. The modelling of the probability of misidentifying a jet as a𝜏had in simulated
events is checked in a control sample requiring𝑍→ℓℓ decays, together with a𝜏had candidate, providing a
sample with more than 90% purity in events containing𝑍 boson and a jet misidentified as𝜏had candidate.
The kinematic distributions are generally well modelled by the simulation, while up to 60% differences are
observed in the overall event yield between data and simulation. These differences include effects from
both the modelling of𝑍+jets and the probability for jets to be misidentified as𝜏had candidates, with the

9 Results
TheTRExFitterframework [94] is used to perform the statistical analysis. Two fit configurations are
employed: one is the ‘background-only’ fit and the other is the ‘signal-plus-background (s+b)’ fit. A
template maximum-likelihood fit [95] is performed using the observed event yields in each region. For the
background-onlyfit,thefoursingle-binCRsareincludedinthefittoconstrainthenormalisationsof 𝑊+jets
and top-quark samples, with a fixed signal strength of𝜇=0 . Separate background normalisation factors
are introduced for the resonance and non-resonance regions, resulting in four independent normalisation
factors: two for𝑊+jets (𝜆Res

top-quark normalisation factors are applied simultaneously to both the𝑡¯𝑡 and single-𝑡 samples. The fit
results are applied to SRs and VRs to compare the observed data with the SM expectation. For the s+b
fit, on the other hand, a fit is performed in the four CR and four SR event categories simultaneously
to constrain the background normalisation and determine the signal strength𝜇. The expected yield is
parameterised in the likelihood as𝜇𝑆+ √𝜇𝐼+𝐵 , where𝑆 denotes the pure BSM contribution,𝐼 denotes
the interference between the BSM and SM𝜏𝜈 amplitudes, and𝐵 denotes the SM background contributions.
The interference term scales with√𝜇, since the pure BSM contribution is quadratic while the interference
is linear in the BSM amplitude. In the SRs with a𝑏-jet veto (SR0b-Resand SR0b-NonRes), the expected
contribution from destructive interference for𝜇=1 amounts to approximately 20% of the purely BSM
yield at𝑚U1 =1.5TeV , increasing to about 30%–40% at higher masses. The impact of interference on
the signal yield is negligibly small in the SRs with a𝑏-jet (SR1b-Resand SR1b-NonRes). It is verified
that, for the range of coupling values considered in this analysis, the total BSM signal plus interference
yield remains positive in all cases. Nuisance parameters are correlated across analysis regions and between
signal and background samples, where applicable, unless stated otherwise. The sensitivity is driven by the
SR1b regions. However, for larger values of𝛽23

reconstructed𝑏-jet and the SR0b regions are sensitive to such𝑈1 signals.

10 Conclusion
This Letter presents the first ATLAS search targeting high-mass𝑈1 leptoquark signals in the𝜏𝜈(+𝑏)
final state with a hadronically decaying𝜏-lepton and missing transverse momentum. The search uses
140 fb−1 of data from 13 TeV proton–proton collisions at the LHC. The analysis is designed to probe both
resonant production and non-resonant𝑡-channel exchange of the leptoquark, covering a wide range of
coupling scenarios. No significant excess is observed over the SM background. Upper limits on the𝑈1
vector-leptoquark couplings are set at 95% CL for masses between 1.5 TeV and 3.0 TeV. The results probe
part of the parameter space at high𝛽23
L values, which is not covered by the previous𝜏𝜏searches.
