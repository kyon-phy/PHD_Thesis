\section{Statistical method}
\label{sec:ch10_statistical_model}

\subsection{Likelihood model}
\label{subsec:ch10_likelihood_model}
The likelihood model is used to test for the presence of a BSM signal. In a maximum-likelihood fit, the model parameters are determined by maximising the likelihood function. This function combines Poisson probabilities for the observed event yields in the fitted regions with constraint terms for the nuisance parameters (NPs):
\begin{align}
 \mathcal{L}(\mu,\vec{\lambda},\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})
 &=\prod_{\mathrm{SR,CR}}\mathrm{Pois}\bigl(N_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid N_{\mathrm{exp}}(\mu,\vec{\lambda},\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})\bigr)
 \nonumber\\
 &\quad\times\prod_k C_k(\theta_k)\,C_{\mathrm{MC}}(\vec{\gamma}),
 \label{eq:ch10_likelihood}
\end{align}
where $\mu$ is the signal strength, $\vec{\lambda}=\{\lambda_W^{\Res},\lambda_W^{\NonRes},\lambda_t^{\Res},\lambda_t^{\NonRes}\}$ contains the background normalisation factors (NFs), $\vec{\gamma}$ denotes the MC statistical NPs, and $\vec{\theta}$ denotes the NPs representing the systematic uncertainties introduced in Chapter~\ref{chapter:systematics_uncertainties}.
$C_k(\theta_k)$ is the constraint term for the corresponding systematic NP and may take a Gaussian, log-normal or Poisson form, depending on the uncertainty.
$C_{\mathrm{MC}}(\vec{\gamma})$ is the product of the auxiliary Poisson constraints for the MC statistical NPs.
Since only the total event yield in each region is used in the fit, the shape systematics are pruned.
The $\bTagSR$-\Res region contributes one bin to the likelihood after the $m_{b\hadtau}>800~\GeV$ requirement because the simulated background statistics at high $m_{b\hadtau}$ are insufficient to support a fit with several mass bins.

The expected yield in each region is given by
\begin{align}
 N_{\mathrm{exp}}(\mu,\vec{\lambda},\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})
 &=\mu N_{\mathrm{sig}}(\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})
   +\sqrt{\mu}\,N_{\mathrm{int}}(\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})
   \nonumber\\
 &\quad+\lambda_W N_W^{\mathrm{MC}}(\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})
   +\lambda_t N_t^{\mathrm{MC}}(\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})
   \nonumber\\
 &\quad+N_{\mathrm{others}}^{\mathrm{MC}}(\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta}),
 \label{eq:ch10_expected_yield}
\end{align}
where $N_{\mathrm{sig}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{int}}$ are the pure-BSM and interference yields, respectively. The remaining terms are the MC background yields, with the $\wjets$ and top-quark contributions scaled by the normalisation factors introduced in Chapter~\ref{chapter:background_estimation}.

The NFs are free parameters that scale the corresponding $\wjets$ and top-quark background yields in both the CRs and SRs.
They are constrained by the observed yields through the Poisson terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch10_likelihood}.

The finite MC sample sizes introduce statistical uncertainties in $N_W^{\mathrm{MC}}$, $N_t^{\mathrm{MC}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{others}}^{\mathrm{MC}}$, including the $Z$+jets and diboson backgrounds.
These are represented by the NPs $\vec{\gamma}$ using the Barlow--Beeston lite prescription~\cite{Conway:2011in}, in which the MC statistical uncertainties of the simulated samples in each bin are combined and represented by one common multiplicative NP in $\vec{\gamma}$.
It changes their contributions to $N_{\mathrm{exp}}(\mu,\vec{\lambda},\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})$ and has an auxiliary Poisson constraint $C_{\mathrm{MC}}(\vec{\gamma})$.

The systematic NPs $\vec{\theta}$ modify the signal and background contributions to $N_{\mathrm{exp}}(\mu,\vec{\lambda},\vec{\gamma},\vec{\theta})$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch10_expected_yield}.
The constraint terms $C_k(\theta_k)$ restrict these variations according to the systematic uncertainties evaluated in Chapter~\ref{chapter:systematics_uncertainties}.
The NFs, MC statistical NPs and systematic NPs are varied together in the fit. The fitted NF uncertainties therefore include the effects of both data statistics and the NPs.

$\sqrt{\mu}=x$ is the parameter of interest (POI) in the analysis since the interference term is scaled by it. $x\geq0$ is therefore required.
The background-only and signal-plus-background (S+B) models correspond to $x=0$ and $x>0$, respectively.

\subsection{Fit strategy}
\label{subsec:ch10_fit_strategy}
The following two fit configurations are used in the analysis:
\begin{itemize} 
  \item \textbf{Background-only fit}: The fit is performed in the CRs to obtain the background estimation without any signal assumption. It fits the four background NFs and the NPs to maximise the likelihood only in the CRs with $x=0$.
  The fitted values for NFs and NPs are used to predict the background yields in the SRs and VRs.
  This fit is also used to assess the constraints on the NPs from the CR data alone.
  \item \textbf{S+B fit}: The fit extends the likelihood fit to the SRs and allows $x$ to vary to determine the best-fit value $\hat{x}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Test statistic and NP impacts}
The test statistic is defined using the profile-likelihood ratio, with the likelihood expressed as a function of $x$.
Profiling means that at each fixed $x$, the background NFs and NPs are fitted again to obtain the maximum likelihood value.
It then compares the maximum likelihood at fixed $x$ with the likelihood at $\hat{x}$.
Thus,
\begin{align*}
 q_x=-2\ln
 \frac{\mathcal{L}(x,\vec{\lambda}_{x},\vec{\gamma}_{x},\vec{\theta}_{x})}
      {\mathcal{L}(\hat{x},\hat{\vec{\lambda}},\hat{\vec{\gamma}},\hat{\vec{\theta}})},
\end{align*}
where $\hat{\vec{\lambda}}$, $\hat{\vec{\gamma}}$ and $\hat{\vec{\theta}}$ are the best-fit values of the background NFs, MC statistical NPs and systematic NPs, respectively. $q_x$ therefore has a minimum value of zero, and a large $q_x$ indicates poor agreement with the tested hypothesis.

Expanding $q_x$ around $\hat{x}$, using $q_{\hat{x}}=0$ and neglecting higher-order terms, gives the local Gaussian approximation:
\begin{align*}
 q_x\simeq\frac{(x-\hat{x})^2}{\sigma_x^2},
\end{align*}
where $\sigma_x$ is the standard deviation of $\hat{x}$.
Within this approximation, the deviation of the fit parameter $x$ from its best-fit value can be quantified by $q_x$.
Specifically, $q_x=0$ means that $x=\hat{x}$, while $q_x=1$ corresponds to a displacement of one standard deviation from $\hat{x}$.
Thus, by scanning $x$ and evaluating the corresponding $q_x$ values, the $\pm1\sigma$ variations of $x$ can be obtained from the points satisfying $q_x=1$.
The same interpretation is applied to the NPs.

The contributions of each NP to the uncertainty on the fitted parameter $x$ are estimated from the post-fit covariance matrix using a local linear approximation:
\begin{align}
 \Delta x_{\mathrm{up}}
 &=\sigma_x^{\mathrm{up}}\rho\sigma_{\mathrm{NP}}^{\mathrm{up}},
 \nonumber\\
 \Delta x_{\mathrm{down}}
 &=-\sigma_x^{\mathrm{down}}\rho\sigma_{\mathrm{NP}}^{\mathrm{down}},
 \label{eq:ch10_np_impact}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_x^{\mathrm{up/down}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{NP}}^{\mathrm{up/down}}$ are the positive magnitudes of the upper/lower post-fit uncertainties in $x$ and the NP, respectively, and $\rho$ is their correlation coefficient.
A larger $|\Delta x|$ indicates a greater impact of the NP on $x$.

\subsection{Hypothesis testing and CL$_{\mathrm{s}}$ method}
Hypothesis testing is performed to determine if the observed data are consistent with the SM-only or SM+BSM scenario.
The null hypothesis refers to the SM-only scenario, and the alternative hypothesis refers to a scenario with the leptoquark signal.
In case no significant deviation is observed from the SM, upper limits are set by the CL$_{\mathrm{s}}$ method with the one-sided statistic
\begin{align*}
 \tilde q_x=\begin{cases}
 q_x &\hat{x}\leq x,\\
 0 &\hat{x}>x.
 \end{cases}
\end{align*}

The tail probabilities of $\tilde q_x$ under the tested S+B and background-only hypotheses are defined as follows~\cite{Read:2002hq}:
\begin{align*}
 \mathrm{CL}_{s+b}&=P(\tilde q_x\geq\tilde q_x^{\mathrm{obs}}\mid x),&
 \mathrm{CL}_{b}&=P(\tilde q_x\geq\tilde q_x^{\mathrm{obs}}\mid0),
\end{align*}
where $\tilde q_x^{\mathrm{obs}}$ is evaluated using the observed data.
Their ratio gives the CL$_{\mathrm{s}}$:
\begin{align*}
 \mathrm{CL}_{s}&=\frac{\mathrm{CL}_{s+b}}{\mathrm{CL}_{b}}.
\end{align*}
$\mathrm{CL}_{s}$ is more robust against background fluctuations compared with $\mathrm{CL}_{s+b}$.
The upper limit on $x$ is then found at $\mathrm{CL}_{s}=0.05$, which corresponds to 95\% confidence level (CL).

\subsection{Limit calculation}
\label{subsec:ch10_limit_calculation}

The 95\% CL upper limit is obtained using $x$ as the parameter of interest and is then converted to a limit on $\mu$ through $\mu=x^2$.
Two methods are applied in this analysis to calculate the upper limit on $x$:

\subsubtitle{Pseudo-experiments}
The calculation uses pseudo-experiments to calculate $\mathrm{CL}_{s}$:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item At each tested $x$, pseudo-datasets are generated under the S+B and background-only hypotheses, with event yields fluctuated according to their Poisson distributions.
 \item Each pseudo-dataset is fitted with $x$ fixed while floating the free parameters (NFs and NPs) to calculate $\tilde q_x$.
 \item The fractions of pseudo-experiments with $\tilde q_x\geq\tilde q_x^{\mathrm{obs}}$ determine the $\mathrm{CL}_{s}$. The calculation is repeated across $x$ values, and the $\mathrm{CL}_{s}=0.05$ crossing is interpolated to obtain the upper limit.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubtitle{Asymptotic approximation}
The pseudo-experiment method gives the test-statistic distributions directly, including the effects of low expected yields, at the cost of computing time.
The asymptotic calculation instead uses analytic formulae to approximate the $\tilde q_x$ distributions~\cite{Cowan:2010js}.
An Asimov dataset is constructed using the expected event yields in the SRs, with NPs and NFs obtained from the background-only fit and a signal scaled by $x^2$.
The tail probabilities are then evaluated from these formulae, and the same $x$ scan is performed.
The calculation with this method is faster and avoids fluctuations from a finite number of pseudo-experiments, but the approximation can be less reliable for expected event yields of $\mathcal{O}(1)$. In this analysis the two methods agree to within a few percent. For example, the upper limit on $\gU$ at $\beta_L^{23}=1.0,\MU=3.0~\TeV$ is $2.69$ with the pseudo-experiment method and $2.66$ with the asymptotic approximation. The two values differ by about $1.1\%$.

The low SR yields therefore motivate the use of pseudo-experiments for the left-handed limits ($\beta_R^{33}=0$, see Section~\ref{subsec:u1_low_energy_fit}), with interference included and 10\,000 pseudo-experiments at each scan point. On the other hand, the right-handed limits ($\beta_R^{33}=-1$) in the $\bTagSR$ are calculated as a supplementary interpretation using the asymptotic approximation without interference.

The NP pulls are defined as:
\begin{align*}
 \mathrm{pull}=\frac{\hat\theta-\theta_{0}}{\Delta\theta},
\end{align*}
where $\theta_{0}$ and $\Delta\theta$ are the nominal NP value and pre-fit uncertainty, respectively. A pull close to zero indicates that the fitted value remains close to its nominal value.
Additionally, the ratio of the post-fit uncertainty to the pre-fit uncertainty, $\Delta\hat\theta/\Delta\theta$, is used to quantify the strength of the constraint on the NP. A ratio below one indicates that the data further constrain the NP.


Since only the total yield in each CR is used in the fit, a predicted background yield change caused by NP variation can be compensated by a change caused by the corresponding NF. This leads to the correlations between the NPs and NFs.
The post-fit correlations between the NPs and NFs are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_corr_bonly}.

The strongest anticorrelation is between the TES detector NP and $\lambda_t^{\NonRes}$, with $\rho\simeq-0.88$.
The TES variation shifts the calibrated $\hadtau$ energies and also propagates these changes to $\met$, so events can move across the selection thresholds.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_cr_np_variations}, with the NFs fixed at their nominal values, the $+1\sigma$ TES detector variation increases the top-quark background yields by $11.2\%$ and $17.4\%$ in $\CRTop$-$\Res$ and $\CRTop$-$\NonRes$, respectively.
The fit can compensate for these increases by reducing the top-quark NFs, explaining the negative correlations.

Similarly, the $+1\sigma$ muon momentum-resolution variation increases the $\wjets$ yields in $\CRW$ as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_cr_np_variations}(\subref{fig:ch10_cr_np_variations_muon}), producing negative correlations with the $\wjets$ NFs, with $|\rho|$ up to $0.52$.
The $+1\sigma$ muon trigger-efficiency variation instead increases the MC trigger efficiency $\epsilon_{\mathrm{MC}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch6_efficiency_sf}, resulting in a reduction of the trigger scale factor and hence the predicted $\wjets$ yields in $\CRW$.
The fit compensates by increasing the $\wjets$ NFs, giving positive correlations of up to $0.33$.
These correlations are included through the post-fit covariance matrix when evaluating the uncertainties in the expected background yields.

\end{align*}

The requirement $x\geq0$ means that the lower uncertainty on $x$ cannot exceed its central value, which can make the upper and lower uncertainties asymmetric.
$\mu$ is then obtained through $\mu=x^2$, which can also introduce asymmetry in the uncertainties.

The data statistical uncertainty dominates for $\MU=1.5~\TeV$, while both statistical and systematic uncertainties are important for $\MU=3.0~\TeV$.
For $\MU=1.5~\TeV$, the largest NP impact comes from the $\wjets$ heavy flavour uncertainty described in Section~\ref{subsec:ch9_wjets}, followed by the $\wjets$ QCD-scale uncertainties.
For $\MU=3.0~\TeV$, the TES detector NP gives the largest impact, followed by the $\wjets$ QCD-scale uncertainties.
The $\CRW$ regions use $e$/$\mu$ in place of $\hadtau$ and apply a $b$-jet veto, so the $\hadtau$ and flavour-tagging uncertainties do not fully cancel when extrapolating to $\bTagSR$.
The $W+\mathrm{HF}/W+\mathrm{LF}$ uncertainty reflects the different jet flavour composition in these regions.
The $\wjets$ QCD scale uncertainties also affect the extrapolation from the CRs to the SRs and have a large impact because $\wjets$ is the dominant background in the SRs.

\section{Exclusion limits}

\subsection{Left-handed scenario}
\label{subsec:ch10_limits_lh}

This scenario corresponds to the pure-left-handed scenario in Section~\ref{subsec:u1_low_energy_fit}, with $\beta_R^{33}=0$.
The contributions of the individual SRs are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_sr_comparison} using asymptotic calculations.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_sr_comparison}(\subref{fig:ch10_sr_comparison_res_nonres}), the resonant SRs provide the stronger sensitivity at low mass, while the non-resonant SRs become more sensitive as on-shell production is suppressed at higher mass.
Their combination gives the best expected limit.
Meanwhile, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_sr_comparison}(\subref{fig:ch10_sr_comparison_sr0b_sr1b}), $\bTagSR$ provides most of the sensitivity, while adding $\bVetoSR$ improves the combined limit particularly towards large $\beta_L^{23}$ and small $\gU$.
This is expected since the larger signal contribution without a $b$-jet would be enhanced by the second-generation coupling.
These comparisons explain the role of each SR. The SR-combined exclusion limits are then obtained with pseudo-experiments.

At $\MU=2.5~\TeV$, the corresponding limits are approximately $4.0\ (3.9)$ for $\beta_L^{23}=0.2$, $2.30\ (2.19)$ for $\beta_L^{23}=1.0$ and $1.22\ (1.18)$ for $\beta_L^{23}=2.2$.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_limits_mass} shows that the sensitivity of this analysis is relatively limited at small $\beta_L^{23}$, where the upper limits on $\gU$ remain above the region favoured by the $B$ anomalies.
The sensitivity improves as $\beta_L^{23}$ increases.
In particular, the limits in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_limits_mass}(\subref{fig:ch10_limits_mass_beta2_2}) approach the favoured region and begin to overlap with it at low mass.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_limits_couplings} illustrates the additional parameter space constrained by this analysis.
At $\MU=1.5~\TeV$, the contribution from resonant production enhances the sensitivity, allowing part of the region favoured by the $B$ anomalies at the $2\sigma$ level to be excluded at large $\beta_L^{23}$.
At higher masses, resonant production is increasingly suppressed, and the exclusion contours lie further from the favoured region.
These results exclude a region at large $\beta_L^{23}$ and small $\gU$ that was not covered by previous $\tau\tau$ searches.

\subsection{Right-handed scenario}
\label{subsec:ch10_rh}

This scenario corresponds to the right-handed scenario in Section~\ref{subsec:u1_low_energy_fit}, with $\beta_R^{33}=-1$ and $\beta_L^{33}=1$.
The signal yields are obtained with the reweighting procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:signal_samples}.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_rh_visible_xsec} compares the observed 95\% CL upper limits on the visible cross-section for $|\beta_R^{33}|=0$ and 1 at $\gU=1$ and $\beta_L^{23}=0.2$, without considering the interference.
It is defined as:
\begin{align*}
 \sigma_{\mathrm{vis}}^{95}=\mu^{95}\sigma A\epsilon,
\end{align*}
where $\sigma$ is the theoretical signal cross-section. $A$ and $\epsilon$ are the signal acceptance and selection efficiency, respectively. $\mu^{95}$ is the observed upper limit on $\mu$.

Compared with the pure-left-handed scenario, the non-resonant contribution is enhanced more strongly than the resonant contribution in the right-handed scenario.
For non-zero $\beta_R^{33}$, an additional contribution to non-resonant $\tau\nu$ production is generated through the $b_R\tau_R$ coupling, while the $c_L\nu_\tau$ coupling is left unchanged.
For resonant production involving the $b\tau$ coupling, the branching fraction is increased by the additional right-handed coupling, but its enhancement is partly offset by the increase in the total LQ width. Therefore the enhancement is smaller than that of the non-resonant contribution.
The visible cross-section limit is therefore lower for $|\beta_R^{33}|=1$ at $\MU=4~\TeV$.

\section{Future improvements}
\label{sec:ch10_future}

The coupling limits are expected to improve only modestly with the larger datasets from Run~3 and the HL-LHC.
An illustrative projection study compares expected limits at $140~\ifb$ with those at $450~\ifb$ and $3000~\ifb$ using Asimov datasets at $\sqrt{s}=13~\TeV$ with the same selections, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch10_lumi_projection}.
The limits are calculated using the asymptotic method with the current configuration, including the four SRs and interference contributions.
The Asimov datasets are constructed from the background predictions obtained by fitting the Run~2 data under the background-only hypothesis.
Both histogram yields and statistical errors are scaled in proportion to the luminosity, keeping the relative MC statistical and systematic uncertainties unchanged.
For $\MU=3.0~\TeV$ and $\beta_L^{23}=1.4$, the expected 95\% CL upper limit on $\gU$ improves by approximately $5\%$ and $10\%$ at $450~\ifb$ and $3000~\ifb$, respectively, compared with $140~\ifb$.
This limited gain is consistent with the approximate $\gU^4$ dependence of the non-resonant pure-BSM contribution at fixed $\MU$ and $\beta_L^{23}$ and the unchanged relative input uncertainties.
Further improvements are therefore expected from reoptimising the selections in addition to increasing the integrated luminosity.

The right-handed interpretation should be extended with interference included in the limit calculation.
For $(\MU,\gU,\beta_L^{23})=(1.5~\TeV,1.0,0.2)$ with $\beta_R^{33}=-1$, the ratios of the interference yield to the pure-BSM yield are $-0.22\pm0.03$ in $\bVetoSR$-\Res and $-0.68\pm0.05$ in $\bVetoSR$-\NonRes. These negative contributions reduce the signal excess and motivate including interference in a future interpretation of these regions.
Additional interference samples covering masses and couplings in the full range of interest are therefore needed.

The sensitivity could be improved through a dedicated optimisation of $\bVetoSR$, whose selections were inherited from the $\bTagSR$.
A preliminary study with selected leading systematic uncertainties found improved sensitivity with $\met>600~\GeV$ in $\bVetoSR$-\Res.
The higher threshold favours events containing an energetic neutrino from an on-shell $U_1\to c\nu_\tau$ decay, while suppressing the softer $\wjets$ background.
The magnitude of the interference compared with the pure-BSM yield is also reduced in such phase space.
This optimisation was not applied in this thesis, and its potential gain should be reassessed in a future analysis.

The supplementary study about the impact of the signal contamination from $\tau\tau$ production is also presented in Appendix~\ref{app:tautau_contamination}. It indicates that omitting the $\tau\tau$ signal contribution slightly underestimates the expected sensitivity by a few percent.
A future analysis should therefore also include all relevant LQ signal contributions, including both $\tau\nu$ and $\tau\tau$ production.
